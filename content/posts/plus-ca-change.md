The first time I heard the phrase *plus ça change, plus c'est la même chose* was in the Rush song ["Circumstances"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i9dHL7GA1nk). While it's not the most notable track from *Hemispheres*, often overshadowed by *La Villa Strangiato*, *The Trees*, or the album's titular *Cygnus X-1, Book II: Hemispheres*, on a record with only 4 songs total it's far from a deep cut. It's a reflective acceptance of the nature of change and the comforting, if not almost pessimistic, realization that while the world around us evolves we are still bound to the grounding reality that we are all along the same selves within. The illusion of time can make it feel like we've come so far from where we started, but we are often merely being tricked by circumstances. I knew enough middle school French at the time to get that the phrase was translated in the next line, *"The more that things change, the more they stay the same"* but I didn't quite grasp the full meaning in the context of the song. Maybe I still don't.

Recently I decided to quote *this song* specifically (not just the common idiom) in a LinkedIn post about AI.

I thought a lot before I posted that. It's cute, snarky, a little apathetic, and it tries too hard to seem like it's not trying hard enough. 10/10 me post. Top it off with distilling the grand abstract concept of illusory change down to a generic pontification on AI and I thought it was perfect for LinkedIn. But I worried a little about the message: will this seem like I'm just admitting to being a fraud? After all these years of coding, hundreds of open source contributions, and presumably career "growth" was I just... copying and pasting the whole time? Especially now when AI scrutiny is so high, the influx of drive-by slop contributions have understandably fueled open source skepticism around using AI. Is this just an admission that I'm not as good as I act like I am? One thing that hasn't changed is my imposter syndrome.

I don't actually know when I started programming, because I don't know what constitutes "starting programming" (and I don't think anyone does). But as long as I've been using computers, I've been making them do things. Does making little games in PowerPoint and Excel count? Learning HTML and CSS on a Freewebs domain? The adolescent script-kiddie phase when I could make the CD drive open randomly with a batch script? The first time I compiled a C++ helloworld with Bloodshed? Or when I deployed my first LAMP stack? I can't point to a single instance any more than I can tell you when I grew from a boy to a man. In fact I might still be a boy today.

But through every phase, I was copying and pasting. When I wasn't copying and pasting from StackOverflow I was copying from other code. When I was learning Kubernetes in 2016, "Google" stopped being a reference because a lot of the stuff I was trying to do hadn't been done anywhere else besides in the nascent Kubernetes codebase. So I copied a lot of code from there.

I wasn't copying code just to ship it and move on. If that was the case, I probably never would have progressed beyond the PowerPoint games in the first place. Copying code served a purpose to help me understand bigger problems. It was always a calculated tradeoff between treating boilerplate or patterns I didn't understand as a black box I could stand on top of to do something new. In a way, it was a form of mental tech debt -- a loan that I was taking out to be paid back later, with interest. And if I was doing what others had done, then I could at least understand that I was on the correct path. Copying wasn't always in the literal sense, sometimes it was just assimilative mimicry. Like when Patrick copied everything Spongebob did so he could win an award.

From that perspective, if I hadn't copied anything I never would have progressed as a programmer either. There's no reason to gatekeep yourself against things you don't fully understand. What does it even mean to "fully understand"? If you truly want to know what your code does, you must first invent the universe. A reasonable concession is to at least know what the functions you're calling do. If you're not treating copied code like something you owe yourself to understand, you're doing yourself a disservice.

I'm not alone in this; everyone has always copied code. Though it's always been frowned upon (and often legally debated) to merely copy and paste without thinking, no one ever blocked any of my code on suspicion of just being copied. Probably because a lot of their code was copied too, making it an ubiquitous practice among professionals and to an extent the very point of open source to begin with. More likely it was because there was a mutual assumption that you understood your code enough to put your name on the blame. That hasn't changed.

What has changed is *how* we copy and paste. Before AI, to even find the code you would copy took some research. Copied code was assembled piecemeal where you already had at least some idea it would fit in. The mere act of copying in and of itself had built in guiding rails along the path to learning. AI, on its face, took that from us.

Obviously my LinkedIn post is a big reduction of what it means to "copy and paste" code and a lot more has changed about me than just how I copy and paste my code. That was the point that I was really trying to make: for a good programmer, the code is just a detail.

But there's a deeper question in all of that. The phases of what I consider my "programming career" weren't all centered around writing code. What they do have in common is solving problems: make this PowerPoint slide go to this one, make this CD drive open, say "hello world". Along the way I started using code more, and more, (and more), but my professional growth doesn't show in lines of code. It shows in the bigger problems I've been able to wrap my head around. So did it really matter where the code came from? Was it ever even about the code?

"Circumstances" was one of my favorite songs to play on guitar. The pre-chorus riff is a technical descending scale crawl, each note striking just shy of staccato, resolving with a satisfying chord brandish. I'm still learning, I'm still copying code, and I still listen to Rush. Plus ca change.
